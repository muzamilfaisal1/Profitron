A separate research thread treats trading as a reinforcement learning problem in which an agent learns to take actions (buy, sell, hold) that maximise a cumulative reward signal corresponding to portfolio value. The motivation is that RL frames the problem in terms that align directly with the economic objective, rather than the proxy objective of directional accuracy that supervised classification optimises. Early work in this area dates back to the late 1990s, but the practical use of deep reinforcement learning in trading became feasible only with the advent of stable training algorithms such as DQN, A2C, and PPO in the mid-2010s.

The most prominent open-source effort in this space is the FinRL library developed by Liu and colleagues, which provides a unified environment for training and evaluating RL trading agents across a range of assets. Empirical results from the FinRL benchmarks suggest that RL agents can match or modestly exceed supervised baselines on backtest data, but several caveats apply. RL training is substantially less stable than supervised training, with the variance across random seeds being a serious obstacle to reproducibility. RL agents are also susceptible to reward-hacking behaviour in which the agent learns to exploit idiosyncrasies of the simulator rather than learn a genuine trading policy; this behaviour does not transfer to live deployment.

We experimented with an RL variant of the present system early in development and abandoned it after observing both of these failure modes in practice. The supervised baseline trained more reliably, generalised more cleanly to live data, and produced an interpretable confidence score that the strategy engine could exploit. The cost of these advantages is that the supervised objective does not directly optimise the economic outcome, and the gap between proxy and true objective is a real source of suboptimality. Future work that combines a supervised pretraining phase with an RL fine-tuning phase may be able to capture the advantages of both approaches; we sketch this direction in Section 8.

2.5 Sentiment Analysis for Financial Prediction

A third research thread proposes that the textual stream of financial news, social media, and analyst reports contains information that complements the numeric price series. The hypothesis is intuitive — surely an unexpected announcement moves prices in ways that historical price data alone does not foresee — but the empirical evidence on whether sentiment signals add predictive power beyond price-and-volume features is mixed.

The strongest results in the sentiment literature have been obtained at daily or weekly horizons and on assets with high media coverage, such as large-cap equities. At minute-scale crypto horizons, the signal-to-noise ratio of public sentiment streams is substantially lower because the volume of irrelevant or low-information posts is high relative to the volume of genuinely market-moving information. We performed a brief experiment in which we incorporated a daily sentiment score derived from a public Twitter feed into our feature vector; the resulting model exhibited slightly lower out-of-sample accuracy than the price-only baseline, which is consistent with the noise-dominance interpretation. We do not include sentiment features in the production system but flag them as a candidate for future work at coarser time horizons.

2.6 Algorithmic Execution and Order Routing

Separately from the modelling literature there is a sizeable body of work on order-execution strategies. Time-weighted average price and volume-weighted average price algorithms, originally developed for institutional block orders, have trickled down to retail contexts as exchange APIs have begun to expose the necessary primitives. Smart order routing across fragmented venues, adaptive slippage control under adverse liquidity, and iceberg-style child-order sequencing are all part of the standard institutional toolkit; the hobbyist literature has been slower to adopt them, despite the fact that the underlying mechanics are straightforward when the execution layer is designed as a first-class component rather than a thin wrapper around a brokerage software development kit.

Empirical studies of live trading routinely show that execution quality dominates model quality in determining realised performance. A directional classifier with sixty percent accuracy coupled to a brute-force market order submitted after observable slippage will typically lose money; the same classifier coupled to a child-order scheduler that monitors fills against a benchmark will generally earn the edge implied by the classification accuracy. The present work takes this observation seriously and makes the execution layer a peer of the forecasting layer rather than a subordinate. In particular, we devote more engineering effort to ensuring that orders are placed with low latency, that their fills are tracked accurately, and that exceptional conditions such as exchange-induced rate limits are handled gracefully than we do to incremental improvements in classifier accuracy beyond the threshold required for positive expectancy.

The microstructure literature on slippage and market impact, while developed primarily for institutional block trades, transfers cleanly to the retail setting once the relevant scale parameters are adjusted. The square-root model of market impact predicts that slippage scales approximately with the square root of order size relative to typical market volume; on retail order sizes this prediction is approximately accurate, with the practical implication that orders below a few hundred dollars exhibit slippage indistinguishable from noise, while orders in the low thousands begin to show measurable impact. The order-slicing logic in Section 4 is parameterised to keep child orders well below this threshold.

2.7 Retail Trading Platforms and Messaging Interfaces

Commercial retail platforms such as MetaTrader, 3Commas, Pionex, and Bitsgap offer bundled forecasting, execution, and alerting, but they impose one of several constraints: a subscription fee, a lock-in to a specific exchange or asset class, a restricted menu of strategies, or a closed source that cannot be audited by the user. Open-source projects, notably Freqtrade, Zipline, Backtrader, and Gekko, cover the execution and backtesting side well but typically leave forecasting to the user and do not provide a polished messaging-based control surface. The result is a fragmented landscape in which a retail trader who wants both a learned forecaster and a polished interface must either pay for a commercial platform that constrains their flexibility or assemble the components themselves at substantial integration cost.

The use of consumer messaging applications as the human-machine interface for trading bots is a comparatively recent development. Telegram has become popular in this role because its Bot API is free, its clients are available across all consumer platforms, and its two-factor authentication can be pressed into service for basic access control. Existing Telegram trading integrations tend to be thin broadcasting channels rather than full agents: they forward signals but rarely close the loop by executing, reporting back, and accepting control commands through the same channel. The architectural gap is an opportunity, and one that the present work attempts to fill.

2.8 Risk Management Frameworks

A separate strand of literature, developed primarily by hedge-fund practitioners and only partially absorbed by the academic community, addresses the design of risk-management frameworks for systematic strategies. The core insight of this literature is that the risk-management layer is at least as important as the prediction layer in determining long-run outcomes, because the loss from a single bad regime can wipe out years of accumulated edge. The standard tools — fixed-fractional position sizing, volatility-scaled stop-loss, daily-loss circuit breakers, and consecutive-loss cool-downs — are individually simple, but their combination requires careful thought to avoid pathological interactions. In particular, a stop-loss whose distance is fixed in price terms behaves very differently across regimes of differing volatility, and a position sizer whose fraction is fixed in percentage terms behaves very differently across regimes of differing trade frequency.

We take seriously the prescription that risk controls should be the first thing designed and the last thing relaxed, and we encode this prescription architecturally by making the risk gates mandatory components in the order path rather than optional overlays. A trade that has not been validated against the current risk parameters cannot be submitted to the exchange, regardless of how confident the forecaster is about the underlying signal.

2.9 Behavioural Finance and Retail Trading Outcomes

A separate but relevant body of literature documents the actual outcomes experienced by retail traders in financial markets. The findings are remarkably consistent across studies and time periods. Barber and Odean's seminal analysis of US discount-brokerage accounts in the 1990s found that the most active retail traders underperformed the relevant index by approximately seven percentage points per year, with most of the underperformance attributable to transaction costs and the remainder to systematically poor timing of trade decisions. Subsequent studies in Taiwan, Korea, China, and several European markets have produced qualitatively similar findings: the average retail trader underperforms a passive benchmark by a meaningful margin, and the most active retail traders underperform by the largest margin.

The mechanisms underlying this underperformance have been studied in detail. Disposition effect — the tendency to close winning positions too early and ride losing positions too long — has been documented across multiple studies and is one of the most robust findings in behavioural finance. Overconfidence bias — the tendency for individual traders to attribute past wins to skill and past losses to luck — drives both excessive trading frequency and excessive position sizing. Anchoring effects — the use of the entry price or some other arbitrary reference point as a basis for exit decisions — produce systematic distortions of exit timing. Loss aversion — the asymmetric weighting of losses relative to gains, documented quantitatively by Kahneman and Tversky — causes traders to take excessive risk to avoid realising losses while shying away from positions whose expected value is positive but variance is high.

The cumulative effect of these biases is that the typical retail trader's realised performance is substantially worse than what their nominal strategy, mechanically applied, would have produced. This observation is the most direct argument for an automated agent: the agent does not exhibit these biases, and an automated implementation of even an imperfect strategy can outperform a human implementation of the same strategy on average. Our pilot data, in which manual overrides reduced realised performance by approximately four percentage points on average, is a small confirmation of this broader literature within the specific context of our system.

2.10 Positioning of the Present Work

In summary, the novelty of our contribution lies less in any individual component than in the composition. We take mature building blocks from the streams above — an LSTM forecaster, a conventional indicator panel, a rule-based execution engine with institutional-style order-handling primitives, a Telegram-based control surface, and a disciplined risk-management framework — and arrange them into a system whose explicit design goal is operability by a single non-institutional user. To our knowledge there is no prior open description of such a system that reports honest regime-conditional evaluation results on live capital. This paper is an attempt to fill that gap.

A useful way to position the contribution is to compare it explicitly to the closest alternatives. The commercial bundled platforms we discussed earlier — 3Commas, Pionex, Bitsgap — provide most of the functionality we describe but at the cost of a subscription fee, source-code opacity, and limited extensibility. The open-source frameworks we discussed — Freqtrade, Backtrader, Zipline — provide most of the execution and backtesting infrastructure but typically leave the forecasting layer to the user and provide little support for messaging-based control surfaces. The academic prototypes that have appeared in the literature provide forecasters of varying sophistication but typically run only in backtest and do not address the operational challenges of live deployment. The present work occupies the intersection of these categories — open-source, end-to-end, evaluated on live capital — that is, to our knowledge, currently unoccupied.

We acknowledge that the boundary between research contribution and engineering work is, in this domain, blurry. Many of the design decisions that we describe are best understood as engineering choices grounded in production experience rather than as scientific contributions in the conventional sense. We have chosen to treat both kinds of decisions as contributions because the academic literature on systematic trading is dominated by accounts that focus on the algorithmic novelty and underplay the operational engineering, while the operational practitioners' literature is dominated by accounts that focus on engineering and underplay the methodological rigour. A unified account that takes both seriously seems to us more useful than another instance of either pure-research or pure-engineering writing, even if the unified account is harder to position cleanly within the academic literature.

3. Problem Formulation

3.1 The Operator's Situation

Independent traders face a recurring dilemma. The markets they wish to participate in operate twenty-four hours a day in the case of crypto or for windows that do not align with other obligations in the case of equities; human attention, meanwhile, is finite and emotionally volatile. Manual trading therefore tends to produce one of two failure modes. Either the trader misses opportunities because they are asleep, commuting, or working on something else; or they over-react to short-term noise because they happen to be watching the chart during a drawdown. Both failure modes are well documented in the behavioural-finance literature and are strongly associated with underperformance relative to even a mediocre mechanical strategy.

The retail trader in an emerging market such as Pakistan has an additional handicap. High-quality data feeds are priced for institutional budgets, brokerage platforms with local fiat onboarding are limited in scope, and the cultural expectation of manual hands-on trading remains dominant. An automated agent that works with the APIs and exchanges available to this audience — Binance, Bybit, Alpha Vantage for equities, and other regionally accessible venues — is therefore particularly valuable. Furthermore, because the absolute amount of capital deployed by the typical retail trader in such markets is modest, even small percentage improvements in realised return translate into meaningful absolute outcomes for the user.

3.2 Formal Problem Statement

We formalise the trading problem as a sequential decision problem in discrete time. At each time step t, the agent observes a state vector s_t containing the historical price series, the technical-indicator panel, and the current portfolio state. The agent then selects an action a_t from a discrete action space consisting of buy, sell, and hold. Each action that opens or closes a position incurs a transaction cost c, which depends on the order size and the prevailing exchange fee schedule. The realised return on the position is determined by the price path that follows the entry and the price path at which the position is closed, and is bounded below by the stop-loss distance and above only by the path realised before the strategy engine decides to close.

The objective is to choose a policy pi mapping states to actions that maximises the expected risk-adjusted return over a long-run horizon. We use the annualised Sharpe ratio as the principal scalar objective, with the maximum drawdown over the evaluation window serving as a hard constraint. Formally, the policy pi-star is defined as the argument maximum, over the space of feasible policies, of the expected Sharpe ratio of the realised return path subject to the constraint that the maximum drawdown does not exceed a configurable threshold.

This formal statement makes explicit the two-stage structure of the optimisation problem. The expected Sharpe ratio is the quantity we want to maximise; the drawdown constraint is the quantity we want to bound. Equivalently, we are looking for the policy that produces the highest risk-adjusted return among the policies that survive the worst-case drawdown bound. The risk-management framework discussed in Section 4 is essentially the operational expression of the drawdown constraint, while the LSTM forecaster and strategy engine together produce candidate policies that maximise the Sharpe-ratio objective subject to the constraint.

3.3 User Personas

Three user personas guided the design of the agent's user interface and feature set. The first is the early-career retail trader, typically aged twenty to thirty, with a few hundred to a few thousand dollars of trading capital and a full-time obligation outside of trading. This persona is technically literate but not a software developer; they can install a Python package and edit a configuration file but cannot debug a stack trace. They want the agent to run reliably without supervision and to surface notifications through a channel they already check, which in practice is a messaging app on their phone.

The second persona is the technically sophisticated retail trader, typically aged thirty to fifty, with a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of dollars of trading capital and either a software-engineering background or a formal quantitative-finance education. This persona wants the same reliability as the first but also wants the ability to extend the agent — to add a new strategy, to integrate a new data source, to plug in a custom risk model. The internal extensibility of the agent — the explicit message-bus boundaries, the well-documented persistence schema, the modular strategy interface — is designed to accommodate this persona.

The third persona is the small-firm proprietary trader, typically a part-owner of a regional brokerage or a small fund, with tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand dollars of trading capital and a strong preference for transparent, auditable trading logic. This persona is unlikely to use the agent as a primary execution venue but may use it as a back-office tool for evaluating ideas, for backtesting strategies, or for exposing controlled trading capability to junior analysts. The data-export and audit-trail features of the agent are designed with this persona in mind.

3.4 Cost Model

The economic feasibility of any retail trading agent depends on the relationship between the agent's expected per-trade gross return and the round-trip transaction cost. We model the round-trip cost as the sum of three components: the explicit exchange fee, the realised slippage against the decision price, and the funding cost (for perpetual futures positions held across funding intervals). On the Binance USDT-margined futures venue we use in our primary deployment, the explicit fee for a small market order is approximately two basis points each way, the realised slippage on retail order sizes is empirically a few basis points, and the funding cost is approximately one basis point per eight hours for typical positions. The total round-trip cost on a typical retail order is therefore approximately five to seven basis points.

This cost model has direct implications for the design of the agent. The strategy engine's confidence threshold and magnitude threshold are set so that the expected value of a trade — the product of the directional accuracy advantage and the expected magnitude of the next move — comfortably exceeds the round-trip cost. A trade whose expected value is below cost is suppressed by the strategy engine even if the directional signal is high-confidence, because executing such a trade is a money-losing proposition in expectation. The threshold parameters are exposed to the user and can be adjusted to reflect the user's specific cost structure.

3.5 What an Agent Must Solve

A software agent that replaces the human in this loop has to do substantially more than issue a forecast. It must, at minimum, solve five interlocking problems. We address each in turn because each shaped the architecture in concrete ways.

First, the agent must maintain a live data pipeline robust to API disconnections, rate limits, and malformed payloads. A naive implementation that polls a REST endpoint every few seconds and prints the last price is a demonstration, not a system; real agents must gracefully handle WebSocket disconnections, exchange-imposed throttling, and the routine chaos of malformed or out-of-order messages that characterises public data feeds. The cost of getting this wrong is not merely an occasional missed update; it is a silently corrupted view of the market that causes the agent to make decisions based on stale or inconsistent state. The mitigations are well known but not always implemented: persistent WebSocket connections with exponential-backoff reconnection, REST polling as a fallback, NTP-synchronised local timestamps, idempotent message handling, and explicit validation of every payload against a schema.

Second, the agent must produce a prediction with an associated confidence score at a cadence fast enough to be actionable but slow enough not to over-trade. Over-trading is a silent source of loss: even small per-trade fees compound quickly when the agent enters and exits positions several times an hour on a thin edge, and the cumulative slippage across many trades easily dominates the modest expected value of any individual trade. The cadence problem is therefore as much an economic problem as a statistical one, and it is one that depends on the per-trade fee structure of the target exchange. For the Binance USDT-margined futures venue we used in our primary deployment, the all-in round-trip cost of a small market order is approximately four basis points; a strategy whose expected value per trade is below five basis points is therefore a money-losing proposition regardless of how accurate the underlying classifier is.

Third, the agent must translate the prediction into an order that respects exposure limits, stop-loss levels, and exchange-specific constraints such as minimum tick size, minimum notional, and lot-size rounding. The gulf between a signal and a filled trade is where most hobby projects silently fail. We have observed open-source bots whose simulated backtest performance was excellent but whose live performance was negative simply because the order-submission logic did not respect the exchange's minimum-notional constraint, causing a non-trivial fraction of intended trades to be silently rejected. A first-class execution layer is therefore not optional; it is the gate that transforms paper performance into realised performance.

Fourth, the agent must persist the resulting state — open positions, closed trades, configuration — in a way that survives a crash. A mid-session segmentation fault that loses track of an open position is a much larger risk than a forecasting error: the position is still open at the exchange, but the agent no longer knows it exists. The standard solution is a write-ahead log with atomic state transitions, which we have implemented, but the implementation requires care to ensure that the log accurately reflects every state change in the order of its occurrence rather than in the order of its acknowledgement.

Fifth, the agent must communicate with the operator in near real time, accepting overrides and confirmations through a channel the operator actually monitors. A web dashboard that is only visible when the user happens to be at the workstation fails the most important test an interface can face. The interface should also be bidirectional: the user must be able to issue commands to the agent — to pause trading, to force a position close, to modify risk parameters — and the agent must be able to acknowledge those commands and report the resulting state.

3.6 Scope and Explicit Non-Goals

We do not attempt to build a high-frequency market-making engine; sub-millisecond latency is outside the scope of the work and would require co-location that is incompatible with the retail setting. We do not attempt to predict black-swan events; the system is explicitly designed to halt trading when volatility exceeds configurable thresholds. We do not attempt to offer regulated financial advice; the agent executes only within the risk parameters the user has set, under a trading account the user already owns, and at the user's sole responsibility. We do not attempt to build a multi-tenant platform; the agent is single-operator by design, and the relaxation of this assumption is a substantial body of additional work that we discuss in Section 7. Finally, we do not attempt to offer comprehensive coverage of every asset class; the system is tuned for cryptocurrency spot and perpetual futures and for large-cap equities, and we report only on its behaviour in those domains.

3.7 Evaluation Criteria

Four families of metrics are used throughout the paper. Predictive metrics cover directional accuracy, precision and recall for each class, ROC and precision-recall curves, and calibration of the confidence scores. Trading metrics cover the equity curve, annualised return, maximum drawdown, Sharpe ratio, Sortino ratio, and win rate. Execution metrics cover fill latency, slippage relative to the decision price, and the fraction of intended orders that were rejected or timed out. Behavioural metrics cover notification delivery reliability and the impact of operator overrides on net performance. Each metric is reported against a relevant baseline, either a passive buy-and-hold strategy or a simpler predictive model of comparable inference cost.

The choice to include behavioural metrics alongside the more conventional families is deliberate. A trading agent that performs admirably in backtest but is so opaque or untrustworthy that the operator overrides it during volatile sessions is, in a meaningful sense, not a successful agent. We have therefore measured the behavioural dimension as carefully as we measured the predictive and execution dimensions, and we discuss the findings in Section 6.

3.8 Threat Model and Robustness Assumptions

Beyond the functional requirements, the agent must be robust to a small but specific set of adversarial conditions. The exchange itself is the principal source of adversarial behaviour, in the sense that exchange-imposed rate limits, IP bans, API key revocations, and forced liquidations are real possibilities that the agent must handle without losing capital. The user's own device is a secondary concern: the agent stores API keys that, if leaked, would allow a third party to drain the user's exchange account, and the storage format must therefore be encrypted at rest with a passphrase that the agent cannot reconstruct on its own. The network is a tertiary concern: WebSocket connections can be hijacked or man-in-the-middled in principle, and the agent uses only TLS-secured connections to mitigate this risk. We do not address the possibility of a fully compromised operating system, on the grounds that such a compromise places the user's entire device at risk and is outside the scope of any single application's security model.

4. Proposed System and Methodology

4.1 Architecture Overview

The system is organised into three cooperating layers, deliberately loosely coupled so that each can be tested or replaced in isolation. Figure 3 sketches the overall decomposition. The data layer owns everything between the exchange socket and a clean tabular feed; the processing layer owns forecasting and decision logic; the interface layer owns the human-facing and exchange-facing surfaces. All three communicate through a shared in-memory message bus, and all three persist their state through a common abstraction to a local SQLite store. The decision to use a publisher-subscriber pattern internally rather than a direct-call architecture was driven primarily by testability: it is straightforward to substitute a mock data source in the data layer and exercise the processing layer in isolation, and equally straightforward to substitute a mock exchange in the interface layer and exercise the rest of the system without risking real capital.

Each layer has its own internal structure that we describe in the subsections below. The layers themselves are stateless with respect to each other in the sense that the data layer does not need to know what the processing layer is doing, and the processing layer does not need to know what the interface layer is doing. State is exchanged only through the message bus and the persistence store, both of which are well-defined boundaries that we can monitor and test.

4.2 Data Acquisition and Preprocessing in Detail
