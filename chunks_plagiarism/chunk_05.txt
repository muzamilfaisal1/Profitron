The internal structure of a canonical LSTM cell is shown in Figure 1. The forget gate decides what proportion of the previous cell state to retain; the input gate and candidate path add new information; and the output gate decides how much of the updated cell state is exposed as the hidden state at the current step. This three-gate structure is the mechanism by which the network preserves useful history across long sequences and suppresses noise that would otherwise accumulate in a simple recurrent unit. The practical effect of these gates is that the network can learn to remember important regime markers — the level of a moving average several minutes ago, the sign of a recent volume spike — for as long as those markers remain useful, and to forget them when they cease to be informative.

Empirical comparisons across the last five years have repeatedly placed LSTM variants ahead of classical econometric baselines such as ARIMA and GARCH, and comfortably ahead of unregularised feed-forward networks, for both equity index futures and the major cryptocurrency pairs. The improvement is typically in the range of three to eight percentage points of directional accuracy, depending on the asset and the evaluation window. Gains over gradient-boosted trees are smaller and less consistent, which is unsurprising given that boosted trees also handle noisy, feature-rich tabular data well. The practical implication is that the choice of model class at this horizon matters less than the choice of features and the integrity of the training protocol.

It is worth distinguishing between two related but distinct claims that often appear in the literature. The first is that LSTMs learn meaningful temporal structure from raw price series. The evidence for this claim is mixed; there is a substantial body of work demonstrating that LSTMs trained only on raw close-price sequences learn little more than a smoothed momentum signal, and that their performance on out-of-sample periods is barely distinguishable from a moving-average baseline. The second, stronger claim is that LSTMs trained on a feature-engineered input — incorporating technical indicators, volume, and volatility measures — can extract patterns that simpler models miss. The evidence for this second claim is considerably stronger and motivates the feature engineering described in Section 4.

2.2 Hybrid and Ensemble Architectures

A more recent strand of research has explored hybrid architectures that combine convolutional layers — applied to a sliding window of candlesticks treated as a two-dimensional surface — with a recurrent layer that consumes the learned embedding. The rationale is that convolutional filters extract short-range spatial patterns (support and resistance structure, volume spikes, candlestick shapes) that the recurrent layer can then contextualise over longer horizons. Reported accuracy gains over pure LSTM baselines are modest, typically a few percentage points, and they come at the cost of additional training data and tuning effort. For cryptocurrency markets, where intra-day volatility is high and regime shifts are frequent, CNN-LSTM hybrids have shown a slightly larger edge than on equity data, which is consistent with the idea that crypto charts contain more exploitable microstructure than mature equity indices.

Parallel to the architectural developments, ensemble methods that blend recurrent networks with tree-based models or with classical statistical forecasters have become popular. The evidence suggests that the value of the ensemble lies less in averaging out noise from any single model than in the ability to dynamically reweight components as market regimes change. A meta-model that tracks recent performance and shifts weight toward the best-performing component during trending regimes and toward a mean-reversion specialist during range-bound regimes is a reliable source of incremental returns. The implicit assumption — that no single specialist is universally best — is borne out by the comparative analysis in Figure 2, which shows the directional accuracy of five candidate models across fourteen major cryptocurrencies. No model is universally dominant, which is the empirical argument for an ensemble meta-model that selects among components based on recent performance.

2.3 Transformer Architectures in Finance

The transformer architecture, having displaced recurrent networks in many other sequence-modelling domains, has begun to appear in financial forecasting research as well. Early results suggest that transformers can match or modestly exceed LSTM performance on long-horizon forecasting tasks, but the inference cost is substantially higher and the training data requirements are non-trivial. For minute-scale retail trading, where inference latency is a hard constraint and where training data is bounded by the practical limits of an exchange's historical API, the case for transformers over LSTMs is not yet decisive. We discuss this trade-off further in Section 8.

The principal technical advantage of transformers over recurrent networks in financial forecasting is their ability to attend to arbitrarily distant points in the input sequence without the gradient-flow constraints that limit recurrent networks. This advantage matters most when the relevant historical context is long — for instance, when forecasting daily or weekly returns based on multiple months of history — and matters less at the minute-scale horizon at which our agent operates, where the relevant history is typically the last few hours. Empirical comparisons by Lim and colleagues, by Wu and colleagues, and by several others have shown that the accuracy gap between LSTMs and transformers narrows substantially at short horizons and that the choice between architectures becomes a matter of inference cost rather than predictive capability.
