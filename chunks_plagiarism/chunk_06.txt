A separate strand of transformer research in finance has explored the use of attention mechanisms to combine multiple data modalities — price series, news text, social media sentiment, on-chain blockchain data — within a single forecasting framework. The results are promising in principle, but the engineering complexity of maintaining the additional data feeds is substantially higher than that of a pure price-series model, and the marginal accuracy gains have, in our reading of the literature, been modest. We considered and rejected this multimodal approach for the present work on the grounds that the operational burden of maintaining the additional feeds outweighs the expected benefit at the scale at which a single retail user operates.

2.4 Reinforcement Learning Approaches to Trading

A separate research thread treats trading as a reinforcement learning problem in which an agent learns to take actions (buy, sell, hold) that maximise a cumulative reward signal corresponding to portfolio value. The motivation is that RL frames the problem in terms that align directly with the economic objective, rather than the proxy objective of directional accuracy that supervised classification optimises. Early work in this area dates back to the late 1990s, but the practical use of deep reinforcement learning in trading became feasible only with the advent of stable training algorithms such as DQN, A2C, and PPO in the mid-2010s.

The most prominent open-source effort in this space is the FinRL library developed by Liu and colleagues, which provides a unified environment for training and evaluating RL trading agents across a range of assets. Empirical results from the FinRL benchmarks suggest that RL agents can match or modestly exceed supervised baselines on backtest data, but several caveats apply. RL training is substantially less stable than supervised training, with the variance across random seeds being a serious obstacle to reproducibility. RL agents are also susceptible to reward-hacking behaviour in which the agent learns to exploit idiosyncrasies of the simulator rather than learn a genuine trading policy; this behaviour does not transfer to live deployment.

We experimented with an RL variant of the present system early in development and abandoned it after observing both of these failure modes in practice. The supervised baseline trained more reliably, generalised more cleanly to live data, and produced an interpretable confidence score that the strategy engine could exploit. The cost of these advantages is that the supervised objective does not directly optimise the economic outcome, and the gap between proxy and true objective is a real source of suboptimality. Future work that combines a supervised pretraining phase with an RL fine-tuning phase may be able to capture the advantages of both approaches; we sketch this direction in Section 8.

2.5 Sentiment Analysis for Financial Prediction

A third research thread proposes that the textual stream of financial news, social media, and analyst reports contains information that complements the numeric price series. The hypothesis is intuitive — surely an unexpected announcement moves prices in ways that historical price data alone does not foresee — but the empirical evidence on whether sentiment signals add predictive power beyond price-and-volume features is mixed.

The strongest results in the sentiment literature have been obtained at daily or weekly horizons and on assets with high media coverage, such as large-cap equities. At minute-scale crypto horizons, the signal-to-noise ratio of public sentiment streams is substantially lower because the volume of irrelevant or low-information posts is high relative to the volume of genuinely market-moving information. We performed a brief experiment in which we incorporated a daily sentiment score derived from a public Twitter feed into our feature vector; the resulting model exhibited slightly lower out-of-sample accuracy than the price-only baseline, which is consistent with the noise-dominance interpretation. We do not include sentiment features in the production system but flag them as a candidate for future work at coarser time horizons.

2.6 Algorithmic Execution and Order Routing

Separately from the modelling literature there is a sizeable body of work on order-execution strategies. Time-weighted average price and volume-weighted average price algorithms, originally developed for institutional block orders, have trickled down to retail contexts as exchange APIs have begun to expose the necessary primitives. Smart order routing across fragmented venues, adaptive slippage control under adverse liquidity, and iceberg-style child-order sequencing are all part of the standard institutional toolkit; the hobbyist literature has been slower to adopt them, despite the fact that the underlying mechanics are straightforward when the execution layer is designed as a first-class component rather than a thin wrapper around a brokerage software development kit.

Empirical studies of live trading routinely show that execution quality dominates model quality in determining realised performance. A directional classifier with sixty percent accuracy coupled to a brute-force market order submitted after observable slippage will typically lose money; the same classifier coupled to a child-order scheduler that monitors fills against a benchmark will generally earn the edge implied by the classification accuracy. The present work takes this observation seriously and makes the execution layer a peer of the forecasting layer rather than a subordinate. In particular, we devote more engineering effort to ensuring that orders are placed with low latency, that their fills are tracked accurately, and that exceptional conditions such as exchange-induced rate limits are handled gracefully than we do to incremental improvements in classifier accuracy beyond the threshold required for positive expectancy.
