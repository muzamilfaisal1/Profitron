The classification head was trained with categorical cross-entropy, the regression head with mean squared error on log-returns, and the combined loss weighted to give the classification task three-quarters of the gradient. The Adam optimiser was used with an initial learning rate of one times ten to the negative third and a cosine-annealing schedule that decays to near zero over fifty epochs. Cosine annealing was chosen after brief experimentation with step decay and exponential decay; the three schedules produced similar terminal accuracy, but cosine annealing delivered the smoothest validation-loss curves and was the easiest to pair with warm restarts in longer runs.

The training data was preprocessed in the obvious ways: prices were transformed to log-returns to stabilise variance across regimes, technical indicators were standardised to zero mean and unit variance using statistics computed only on the training fold, and the resulting feature vectors were normalised within each sample window to remove residual scale dependence. Class labels were assigned by thresholding the next-period return: returns above a small positive threshold were labelled "up", returns below a small negative threshold were labelled "down", and returns within the band were labelled "flat". The thresholds were chosen so that the three classes were approximately balanced over the training window, which simplified the loss-weighting analysis.

The label-smoothing regularisation technique was applied to the categorical cross-entropy loss with a smoothing factor of 0.05. Label smoothing distributes a small fraction of the probability mass from the correct class to the incorrect classes, preventing the model from becoming over-confident in its predictions and improving calibration of the resulting confidence scores. The choice of 0.05 was empirical; smaller values produced models with sharper but less calibrated predictions, and larger values produced models whose predictions were too diffuse to drive useful trading decisions.

We also experimented with several data-augmentation strategies during training, including time-warp transformations of the input window and additive Gaussian noise injection on the input features. Neither augmentation produced a statistically significant improvement on the validation slice, and we chose to omit them from the production training pipeline to avoid the additional complexity. The negative result is itself informative: it suggests that the model is not data-starved in the way that augmentation typically helps mitigate, and that further accuracy gains will need to come from feature engineering rather than from training-data manipulation.

A separate consideration during training was the management of the class imbalance that emerges in non-trending market windows. When a particular training fold happens to contain a long range-bound period, the flat class becomes over-represented relative to the directional classes, which biases the model toward predicting flat. We addressed this by computing per-class weights inversely proportional to the empirical class frequency on each fold, which in practice gives the directional classes higher loss weight during folds with long range-bound periods. The reweighting is applied only at training time; at inference time the model produces unweighted probabilities that the strategy engine then thresholds.

4.11 Hyperparameter Selection

The principal hyperparameters of the LSTM — the number of hidden units per layer and the dropout rate — were selected by a coarse grid search on a held-out validation slice. Figure 10 summarises the outcome. Fifty units with 0.2 dropout produced the best validation accuracy, a result consistent with prior work on similar horizons. The gradient of the surface is shallow in the neighbourhood of the optimum, suggesting that exact tuning is less important than being somewhere in a reasonable region, but the edges of the surface (zero dropout, or very large hidden state) degrade sharply.

The shallow gradient observation has practical implications for users who wish to retrain the model on a different asset or a different time horizon. The conventional advice in such cases is to perform a fresh hyperparameter search; our experience is that the default values continue to perform well across reasonably similar settings, and that the dominant risk is not suboptimal hyperparameters but suboptimal feature engineering or insufficient training data. We therefore recommend that users start with the default values, evaluate the resulting model, and only resort to hyperparameter search if the default-tuned model exhibits clear pathological behaviour.

4.12 Feature Attribution

To understand which features the model actually relies on, we fit a gradient-boosted surrogate on the same input vector used by the LSTM and inspect its feature-importance scores. The single most informative feature is the one-lag close; the RSI and volume features together contribute approximately a quarter of the total importance; and the remaining indicators contribute in a long tail. The finding reassures us that the model is not over-reliant on any one exotic feature, but it also flags that the bulk of the signal comes from very recent price history, which sets a practical limit on how far ahead useful forecasts can be made.
