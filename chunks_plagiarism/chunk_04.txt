The questions this work attempts to answer are as follows. Can a sequence-model forecaster of modest capacity, trained on a practical rather than an academically generous dataset, produce directional signals whose quality is sufficient for risk-adjusted outperformance in both equity and cryptocurrency markets? Can the execution loop downstream of the forecaster be built from freely available components without surrendering the latency and reliability properties usually associated with professional trading infrastructure? Can the system be exposed through an interface that a non-technical operator actually uses, without loss of the control surface required during volatile sessions? And can the entire stack — forecasting, execution, interface — be operated by a single human at a cost that is negligible in absolute terms? We do not claim a definitive answer to any of these in a single six-month evaluation, but we do provide measurable evidence for each, and we organise the remainder of the paper around the dimensions along which that evidence was collected.

1.6 Contributions

The contributions of this work are as follows.

A reproducible blueprint for integrating an LSTM-based forecasting pipeline, a rule-based execution engine, and a messaging-based control surface under one Python runtime, with explicit attention to the cross-cutting concerns that dominate real-world operation.
A publisher/subscriber decomposition of the agent into three layers — data, processing, and interface — that can be developed, tested, and replaced independently, avoiding the monolithic scripting style that characterises most publicly available retail trading bots.
An explicit treatment of risk controls — hard stop-loss, volatility-scaled position sizing, and circuit-breaker logic — as first-class architectural citizens rather than afterthought wrappers around the forecaster.
A candid empirical account of the regime-dependent performance of the resulting system, including the conditions under which the predictive layer outperforms simple heuristics and the conditions under which it does not.
A catalogue of practical failure modes observed during live deployment — exchange API instability, data decay, emotional overrides by the operator — and the mitigations that were most effective against each.
A discussion of the boundary between what can be automated and what must remain in human hands, which we believe is the more interesting scientific output of this kind of system-building project than any single performance metric.

1.7 Reading Guide

Different readers will benefit from different paths through the paper. A reader interested primarily in the system architecture and engineering decisions can read Sections 1, 4, 6, and 7 and skim the remainder. A reader interested primarily in the empirical results can read the abstract, Section 5, and the relevant subsections of Section 6. A reader interested in the broader research questions raised by the work — what is the appropriate division of labour between human and machine in trading, how should retail systems be evaluated, how rapidly do retail-accessible signals decay — should read Sections 1, 6, 7, and 8. A reader interested in reproducing the system or building a comparable one should read all sections, with particular attention to the methodological detail in Section 4.

We have aimed for completeness rather than concision in the body of the paper because we believe the difficulty of building a system of this kind is in the details, and a paper that omits the details would not give a practitioner enough information to actually build the comparable system. This decision lengthens the paper considerably but, we hope, makes it more useful as a reference document.

1.8 Organisation of the Paper

Section 2 surveys the adjacent literature, grouping prior work into four loosely overlapping streams and identifying the gap our system is positioned to fill. Section 3 formalises the practical problem and the scope of the contribution. Section 4 presents the three-layer architecture and the methodology used to train and validate the components. Section 5 reports experimental observations, including forecasting accuracy, trading performance, regime-conditional behaviour, microstructure metrics, and statistical properties of the realised returns. Section 6 discusses the findings, comparing them against published baselines where available and against the qualitative reports of pilot users where comparison with literature is impossible. Section 7 enumerates the limitations we encountered, paying particular attention to the boundary between what was a limitation of the implementation and what appears to be an inherent limitation of the retail setting. Section 8 concludes with a discussion of directions for future work, including transformer-based forecasting, reinforcement learning, and decentralised execution.

2. Related Work

2.1 Sequence Models for Financial Forecasting

The application of machine learning to financial time series predates modern deep learning by several decades, but the availability of retail-grade APIs and open-source neural network libraries has shifted the locus of innovation toward end-to-end systems rather than isolated models. Within the sequence-modelling literature, recurrent architectures have become the default choice for directional prediction at the minute-level or tick-level horizons that concern retail algorithmic traders. The appeal is straightforward: financial time series exhibit long-range dependencies — fading momentum, slow reversion, cross-asset co-movements — that feed-forward architectures struggle to capture without careful feature engineering, whereas recurrent networks, and in particular Long Short-Term Memory networks, retain hidden state across many time steps and can model these dependencies with relatively little manual intervention.
