Second, the agent must produce a prediction with an associated confidence score at a cadence fast enough to be actionable but slow enough not to over-trade. Over-trading is a silent source of loss: even small per-trade fees compound quickly when the agent enters and exits positions several times an hour on a thin edge, and the cumulative slippage across many trades easily dominates the modest expected value of any individual trade. The cadence problem is therefore as much an economic problem as a statistical one, and it is one that depends on the per-trade fee structure of the target exchange. For the Binance USDT-margined futures venue we used in our primary deployment, the all-in round-trip cost of a small market order is approximately four basis points; a strategy whose expected value per trade is below five basis points is therefore a money-losing proposition regardless of how accurate the underlying classifier is.

Third, the agent must translate the prediction into an order that respects exposure limits, stop-loss levels, and exchange-specific constraints such as minimum tick size, minimum notional, and lot-size rounding. The gulf between a signal and a filled trade is where most hobby projects silently fail. We have observed open-source bots whose simulated backtest performance was excellent but whose live performance was negative simply because the order-submission logic did not respect the exchange's minimum-notional constraint, causing a non-trivial fraction of intended trades to be silently rejected. A first-class execution layer is therefore not optional; it is the gate that transforms paper performance into realised performance.

Fourth, the agent must persist the resulting state — open positions, closed trades, configuration — in a way that survives a crash. A mid-session segmentation fault that loses track of an open position is a much larger risk than a forecasting error: the position is still open at the exchange, but the agent no longer knows it exists. The standard solution is a write-ahead log with atomic state transitions, which we have implemented, but the implementation requires care to ensure that the log accurately reflects every state change in the order of its occurrence rather than in the order of its acknowledgement.

Fifth, the agent must communicate with the operator in near real time, accepting overrides and confirmations through a channel the operator actually monitors. A web dashboard that is only visible when the user happens to be at the workstation fails the most important test an interface can face. The interface should also be bidirectional: the user must be able to issue commands to the agent — to pause trading, to force a position close, to modify risk parameters — and the agent must be able to acknowledge those commands and report the resulting state.

3.6 Scope and Explicit Non-Goals

We do not attempt to build a high-frequency market-making engine; sub-millisecond latency is outside the scope of the work and would require co-location that is incompatible with the retail setting. We do not attempt to predict black-swan events; the system is explicitly designed to halt trading when volatility exceeds configurable thresholds. We do not attempt to offer regulated financial advice; the agent executes only within the risk parameters the user has set, under a trading account the user already owns, and at the user's sole responsibility. We do not attempt to build a multi-tenant platform; the agent is single-operator by design, and the relaxation of this assumption is a substantial body of additional work that we discuss in Section 7. Finally, we do not attempt to offer comprehensive coverage of every asset class; the system is tuned for cryptocurrency spot and perpetual futures and for large-cap equities, and we report only on its behaviour in those domains.

3.7 Evaluation Criteria

Four families of metrics are used throughout the paper. Predictive metrics cover directional accuracy, precision and recall for each class, ROC and precision-recall curves, and calibration of the confidence scores. Trading metrics cover the equity curve, annualised return, maximum drawdown, Sharpe ratio, Sortino ratio, and win rate. Execution metrics cover fill latency, slippage relative to the decision price, and the fraction of intended orders that were rejected or timed out. Behavioural metrics cover notification delivery reliability and the impact of operator overrides on net performance. Each metric is reported against a relevant baseline, either a passive buy-and-hold strategy or a simpler predictive model of comparable inference cost.

The choice to include behavioural metrics alongside the more conventional families is deliberate. A trading agent that performs admirably in backtest but is so opaque or untrustworthy that the operator overrides it during volatile sessions is, in a meaningful sense, not a successful agent. We have therefore measured the behavioural dimension as carefully as we measured the predictive and execution dimensions, and we discuss the findings in Section 6.
