The cumulative effect of these biases is that the typical retail trader's realised performance is substantially worse than what their nominal strategy, mechanically applied, would have produced. This observation is the most direct argument for an automated agent: the agent does not exhibit these biases, and an automated implementation of even an imperfect strategy can outperform a human implementation of the same strategy on average. Our pilot data, in which manual overrides reduced realised performance by approximately four percentage points on average, is a small confirmation of this broader literature within the specific context of our system.

2.10 Positioning of the Present Work

In summary, the novelty of our contribution lies less in any individual component than in the composition. We take mature building blocks from the streams above — an LSTM forecaster, a conventional indicator panel, a rule-based execution engine with institutional-style order-handling primitives, a Telegram-based control surface, and a disciplined risk-management framework — and arrange them into a system whose explicit design goal is operability by a single non-institutional user. To our knowledge there is no prior open description of such a system that reports honest regime-conditional evaluation results on live capital. This paper is an attempt to fill that gap.

A useful way to position the contribution is to compare it explicitly to the closest alternatives. The commercial bundled platforms we discussed earlier — 3Commas, Pionex, Bitsgap — provide most of the functionality we describe but at the cost of a subscription fee, source-code opacity, and limited extensibility. The open-source frameworks we discussed — Freqtrade, Backtrader, Zipline — provide most of the execution and backtesting infrastructure but typically leave the forecasting layer to the user and provide little support for messaging-based control surfaces. The academic prototypes that have appeared in the literature provide forecasters of varying sophistication but typically run only in backtest and do not address the operational challenges of live deployment. The present work occupies the intersection of these categories — open-source, end-to-end, evaluated on live capital — that is, to our knowledge, currently unoccupied.

We acknowledge that the boundary between research contribution and engineering work is, in this domain, blurry. Many of the design decisions that we describe are best understood as engineering choices grounded in production experience rather than as scientific contributions in the conventional sense. We have chosen to treat both kinds of decisions as contributions because the academic literature on systematic trading is dominated by accounts that focus on the algorithmic novelty and underplay the operational engineering, while the operational practitioners' literature is dominated by accounts that focus on engineering and underplay the methodological rigour. A unified account that takes both seriously seems to us more useful than another instance of either pure-research or pure-engineering writing, even if the unified account is harder to position cleanly within the academic literature.

3. Problem Formulation

3.1 The Operator's Situation

Independent traders face a recurring dilemma. The markets they wish to participate in operate twenty-four hours a day in the case of crypto or for windows that do not align with other obligations in the case of equities; human attention, meanwhile, is finite and emotionally volatile. Manual trading therefore tends to produce one of two failure modes. Either the trader misses opportunities because they are asleep, commuting, or working on something else; or they over-react to short-term noise because they happen to be watching the chart during a drawdown. Both failure modes are well documented in the behavioural-finance literature and are strongly associated with underperformance relative to even a mediocre mechanical strategy.

The retail trader in an emerging market such as Pakistan has an additional handicap. High-quality data feeds are priced for institutional budgets, brokerage platforms with local fiat onboarding are limited in scope, and the cultural expectation of manual hands-on trading remains dominant. An automated agent that works with the APIs and exchanges available to this audience — Binance, Bybit, Alpha Vantage for equities, and other regionally accessible venues — is therefore particularly valuable. Furthermore, because the absolute amount of capital deployed by the typical retail trader in such markets is modest, even small percentage improvements in realised return translate into meaningful absolute outcomes for the user.

3.2 Formal Problem Statement

We formalise the trading problem as a sequential decision problem in discrete time. At each time step t, the agent observes a state vector s_t containing the historical price series, the technical-indicator panel, and the current portfolio state. The agent then selects an action a_t from a discrete action space consisting of buy, sell, and hold. Each action that opens or closes a position incurs a transaction cost c, which depends on the order size and the prevailing exchange fee schedule. The realised return on the position is determined by the price path that follows the entry and the price path at which the position is closed, and is bounded below by the stop-loss distance and above only by the path realised before the strategy engine decides to close.
